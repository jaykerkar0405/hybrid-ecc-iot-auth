\begin{abstract}
Al-Rasheed et al.\ proposed a two-message hybrid ECC/symmetric-key mutual-authentication protocol for the Internet of Things and reported roughly 17\%, 21\% and 12\% lower communication, processing and storage cost than prior schemes. Those figures rest on OMNeT++ simulation and assumed per-operation times, and no code was published. We audit them. (i)~Their stated percentages cannot be derived from their own tables or figures under any definition we tried, and their tables contradict their text (four messages and 1024 bits versus two messages and 264 bits). (ii)~We could not reproduce their processing figure, and we make no claim about whether it is correct. (iii)~We implement the device side in C, interoperate it with an independent Python server on an ESP8266, and time the primitives on that device, an AWS Graviton2 core and a laptop. P-256 scalar multiplication takes 276~ms on the ESP8266, sixteen times the assumed 17.1~ms, while symmetric operations are five to twelve times faster than assumed. (iv)~With measured values, the protocol is about 60--2500$\times$ cheaper than the ECC-based schemes compared, but it costs more than the hash-only schemes (about 4--14$\times$ on the ESP8266) once the session-key derivation required by its own equations is counted (operation counts verified against independent papers for two such schemes, and against a survey table for the third). The scheme also lacks forward secrecy, uses no elliptic-curve operation online, and has a server-side pseudonym lookup that grows with the number of devices. All code and raw data are open.
\end{abstract}

Internet of Things, mutual authentication, elliptic curve cryptography, reproducibility, microcontroller benchmarking, lightweight security.

\section{Introduction}
Lightweight authentication schemes for the Internet of Things (IoT) are usually compared by counting cryptographic operations and multiplying by assumed per-operation times. The conclusion of such a comparison then depends on a few constants that are rarely measured on the class of device the scheme targets. Al-Rasheed et al.~\cite{base} propose a hybrid scheme in which elliptic-curve cryptography is confined to an offline registration phase and every online authentication uses a two-message nonce-based symmetric handshake. They report about 17\%, 21\% and 12\% lower communication, processing and storage cost than ten prior schemes, using OMNeT++ simulation and the reference times of the base paper's Table~III (an ECC scalar multiplication at 17.1~ms, a symmetric encryption or decryption at 5.6~ms, a SHA-1 hash at 0.32~ms).

We test what can be tested. We do not claim that the authors' simulated results are wrong: where their evidence is unavailable we say so and stop. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A consistency and reproducibility audit} of the paper's tables, equations and figures, including checks of the cost rows against three of the original papers it draws on (Sections~\ref{sec:audit} and~\ref{sec:fig1}).
\item \textbf{Measured primitive costs} on a 32-bit microcontroller (ESP8266), an AWS Graviton2 core and a laptop, using one C source on every platform with checksum-verified outputs, and a recomputation of the paper's cost model with a sensitivity analysis (Sections~\ref{sec:constants} and~\ref{sec:ranking}).
\item \textbf{Protocol-level findings} that bear on how the scheme should be compared: no forward secrecy, no online elliptic-curve operation, and a server lookup cost that grows with the number of devices (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
\item An \textbf{open implementation and benchmark}: a Python reference of the protocol, a portable C device core, ESP8266 firmware, and the audit scripts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Base Protocol and Its Evidence}
\label{sec:base}
In the offline phase a trusted authority (TA) derives a long-term secret $\lambda_X = H_1(\mathrm{ID}_X\,\|\,s) \bmod n$ for each device and server (Eq.~(8) of~\cite{base}) and distributes peer secrets, so that both sides can compute a pairwise root $k^\star_{ij}=\mathrm{KDF}(\lambda_{V_i}\,\|\,\lambda_{R_j})$ (Eq.~(9)). Online, a device sends $M_1=\langle \mathrm{PID}_{V_i,t}, C_1,\mu_1\rangle$ with $\mathrm{PID}=H_2(\lambda_{V_i}\,\|\,t)$, $C_1=\mathrm{Enc}_{k^\star}(\mathrm{PID}\,\|\,N_i\,\|\,t)$ and a MAC $\mu_1$; the server replies with $M_2$, and both derive the session key $K=\mathrm{KDF}(k^\star\,\|\,N_i\,\|\,N_j\,\|\,t)$ (Eq.~(16)). The instantiation notes name AES-GCM~\cite{gcm}, HKDF~\cite{hkdf} and SHA-256. The online cost is stated as one KDF, one encryption and one MAC on the device (Eq.~(22)).

Cost claims rest on the base paper's Tables~IV--VI, which compare the proposed scheme with schemes labelled \cite{porambage,turkanovic,dhillon,jiang,challa,sadhukhan,fakroon} and others, and on the base paper's Figs.~1--3, which compare against four different schemes. Processing cost (the base paper's Table~V) is a per-role count of hash ($T_H$), symmetric ($T_{SE/D}$), ECC scalar-multiplication ($T_{ECM}$) and fuzzy-extraction ($T_{fe}$) operations, multiplied by the base paper's Table~III constants.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Audit}
The base paper's Tables~III--VI were transcribed from 300-DPI renders of the published PDF and re-read at 600~DPI. The base paper's Tables~III, IV and VI were additionally checked by optical character recognition (every cell matched); the base paper's Table~V, whose mathematical typography defeats OCR, was checked by two manual cell-by-cell readings. Bar heights in the base paper's Figs.~1--3 were read by eye from 600-DPI renders (reading error about $\pm0.2$ axis units). For each figure and table we computed the reduction of the proposed scheme under nine definitions (against the best, mean, median and worst competitor, averaged over the $x$-axis, over all scheme/$x$ pairs, at the first and last point, and by ratio of sums).

\subsection{Implementation}
We implemented the nonce-based model of the base paper's Section~III in Python. (Section~II of the base paper describes a different exchange, Algorithms~1--2, whose hash digest and random number are static and carry no per-session nonce, so a recorded exchange can be replayed. Section~III of the base paper has the nonces, the security sketch and the cost equation Eq.~(22), so it is the version we implement; our trusted authority derives $\lambda_X$ locally as in Eq.~(8) and does not run the ECDH registration of Eqs.~(1)--(5).) The implementation uses the \texttt{cryptography} package (AES-256-GCM, HKDF-SHA256, SHA-256), with a length-prefixed JSON/base64 wire format. We then wrote a portable C device core on BearSSL~\cite{bearssl} (the ESP8266 Arduino core ships a port of BearSSL) with the identical wire format and ran it (i) natively against the unmodified Python server (12/12 handshakes) and (ii) on a NodeMCU ESP8266 (Tensilica L106, 80\,MHz, about 80\,KB RAM, no cryptographic hardware) over Wi-Fi (20/20 handshakes). In every case the session-key fingerprints on the two sides were identical and a replayed $M_1$ was rejected. On the ESP8266 a handshake costs 1.67~ms to build $M_1$ and 3.59~ms to verify $M_2$ and derive the key (5.26~ms of device compute, mean of 20), and 52~ms end to end over Wi-Fi; $M_1$ is 216~bytes.

\subsection{Primitive benchmark}
A single C benchmark, built from the same source on every platform, times SHA-1 and SHA-256, HMAC, HKDF-SHA256, AES-256-GCM (with the key schedule paid or cached; three BearSSL AES implementations: bitsliced constant-time \texttt{aes\_ct}, small-table \texttt{aes\_small} and fast-table \texttt{aes\_big}), bare AES and AES-CTR, and P-256 and X25519 scalar multiplication. Platforms: (a) ESP8266 at 80 and 160\,MHz, Wi-Fi off, cycle-counter timing; (b) an AWS t4g.nano (Graviton2/Neoverse-N1), one pinned core; (c) an Apple-silicon laptop; (d) on the laptop, OpenSSL~\cite{openssl} libcrypto, which uses the hardware AES and SHA instructions, timed per message (fresh IV, tag, cached key) with low-level SHA-1 and a low-level HKDF verified equal to OpenSSL's. Each value is the median of 100 runs (24 for elliptic-curve operations). Every output (hashes, ciphertext and tags, curve points) is checksummed; all 41 digests are identical on all BearSSL platforms and no operation reported failure.

\subsection{Recomputing the cost model}
We keep the paper's per-role operation counts (the base paper's Table~V) and replace the base paper's Table~III times by measured ones. Because the count in the base paper's Table~V for the proposed scheme ($2T_H+2T_{SE/D}$ per side) omits the session-key derivation of Eqs.~(16) and~(22), we evaluate four readings: \textbf{V1}~the base paper's Table~V as printed; \textbf{V2}~the base paper's Table~V plus one KDF per side; \textbf{V3}~Eq.~(22) with an AEAD (device: one KDF and one AEAD operation; server: one KDF and two AEAD operations); \textbf{V4}~the ``$2T_H$'' of the base paper's Table~V read as the pseudonym hash plus the KDF (the reading most favourable to the paper). We also sweep how $T_{SE/D}$ is defined (full GCM, GCM with cached key, CTR without MAC, one AES block) and $T_H$ (one or two SHA-1 blocks). Competitor protocols are \emph{not} re-implemented: their cost is the paper's operation counts times our measured times.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Consistency of the paper's evidence}
\label{sec:audit}
Items 1--5 follow from the paper's own text, tables and figures alone; items 6--7 compare its rows with the original papers.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The headline percentages are not derivable.} Under none of nine definitions do the base paper's Tables~IV--VI or Figs.~1--3 give 17\%, 21\% or 12\% (within one point). The base paper's Table~IV gives 36.0\% fewer bits than the best competitor and 57.5\% fewer than the mean; the base paper's Table~VI gives 10.1\% (device) and 33.3\% (server) less storage than the best; the base paper's three figures give 24--49\%, 19--31\% and 7--25\% depending on definition.
\item \textbf{Tables contradict the text.} The base paper's text, Eq.~(21) and Section~V-B state two messages and 264~bits; the base paper's Table~IV lists the proposed scheme with four messages and 1024~bits. Two 128-bit messages total 256, not 264, and a SHA-256 pseudonym (256 bits) plus a 128-bit GCM tag already exceed 264 bits in $M_1$ alone.
\item \textbf{Under the paper's own constants it is not the cheapest.} With the base paper's Table~III, the base paper's Table~V costs the proposed scheme at 23.7~ms; three rows costed with hash operations only come to 6.1, 7.0 and 7.0~ms (all roles).
\item \textbf{Internal inconsistencies.} The base paper's Table~V charges $2T_H+2T_{SE/D}$ per side whereas Eq.~(22) states one KDF, one encryption and one MAC; Algorithms~1--2 describe a different (MAC-address and hash-digest) protocol than the base paper's Sections~III--V; the base paper's Table~VI is captioned ``communication cost'' but reports storage; two schemes share reference number~17 of the base paper; the base paper's Table~II lists servers as ``5\% (one per hundred devices)'' (5\% is one per twenty) and a ``packet size'' of 128~Kbps (a rate).
\item \textbf{The figures disagree with the text.} The base paper's Fig.~1 is captioned ``processing'' but its axis reads ``communication overhead''; the base paper's Figs.~1--2 plot against ``vehicles in VANET'' (300--1800) although the paper targets static IoT devices; the base paper's Fig.~2 plots 22--41 ``bits'' against 264 or 1024 in the text and table, rising with the number of vehicles for a fixed two-message protocol; two series in the base paper's Fig.~1 are clipped at the axis limit; and the base paper's Figs.~1--3 compare against four schemes (Gope, Gupta, Hassan, Zhanfei) that appear in none of the base paper's Tables~IV--VI and in neither the reference list nor the text.
\item \textbf{Row labels do not match the reference list.} The base paper's rows ``Cheng and Le'', ``Jiang et al.'' and ``Shuai et al.'' carry reference numbers 14, 15 and 16 that, in the base paper's own reference list, belong to Wang et al.\ (emergency-vehicle VANET authentication), Wen et al.\ (an edge-AI survey article) and Jiang et al.\ (a chaotic-map three-factor scheme); the row ``Wazid et al.'' carries number 17, which points to Challa et al. Against the original papers we had available (Chang and Le~\cite{changle}, Dhillon and Kalra~\cite{dhillon}, Jiang et al.~\cite{jiang}): the ``Cheng--Le'' row matches Chang and Le's second protocol with the user and gateway hash counts exchanged; the rows ``Jiang'' (7/5/10 hash operations) and ``Shuai'' (hash plus one ECC operation per role) match none of these three, and Jiang et al.'s chaotic-map scheme contains no ECC operation. Two secondary tables suggest their sources. A 2025 survey~\cite{zhong} lists a different paper, the two-factor scheme of Jiang et al.~\cite{jiang2}, at 7/10/5 hash operations (user/gateway/sensor). Banerjee et al.~\cite{banerjee} list Shuai et al.~\cite{shuai} at $6T_h{+}1T_m$ (user), $7T_h{+}1T_m$ (gateway) and $3T_h$ (device) with 296 bytes (2368 bits, the base paper's Table~IV total for that row). The paper's row charges the device one further ECC operation; the printed total of~\cite{banerjee}, $16T_h{+}3T_m$, disagrees with its own rows, which sum to $2T_m$. We could not read the original papers, so these matches are leads, not verifications.
\item \textbf{The cost environment differs from that of the compared papers.} The base paper's Table~III sets the chaotic-map cost $T_C$ equal to $T_H$ (0.32~ms); Jiang et al.\ state $T_H=0.20$~ms and $T_C=32.40$~ms (a factor 162). Their scheme is therefore not hash-only, and costing it with its own constants changes it from cheaper than the proposed scheme to far dearer (Section~\ref{sec:ranking}). Separately, the base paper's Table~V lists Turkanovi\'c et al.\ as 7/7/5 hash operations (user/device/server), whereas the two papers that tabulate it~\cite{changle,dhillon} give 7/5/7; the total is unchanged but device-only and server-only comparisons are not.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The base paper's Fig.~1 could not be reproduced}
\label{sec:fig1}
The base paper's Fig.~1 is the paper's only processing-cost evidence that is not a restatement of the base paper's Table~V, and it underlies the 21\% claim. We tried to reproduce it, with a fixed budget, and could not. The paper states that costs ``were computed in OMNeT++'' (Section~V of the base paper) and later that the scheme was simulated in SUMO (Section~VI of the base paper), but gives no scenario, mobility trace, message sizes, timing model or code, and the article has no data-availability statement. We searched the web for a repository, dataset or supplementary material (two passes, October 2026; the publisher's page was not accessible to us) and found none, and we have not contacted the authors. The simulation is therefore \emph{not publicly available}; we do not claim it does not exist.

We also tried the one thing available to us: a deterministic cost model of the kind the paper describes (operation counts times constants), which gives a per-authentication cost independent of the number of vehicles, so its curves would be flat. The curves digitised from the base paper's Fig.~1 rise by 5--21 axis units over 300--1800 vehicles, and a flat or two-parameter affine fit leaves residuals of 10--36 and up to 20 times our reading error respectively (\path{research/paper_audit/fig1_repro.py}). We read this only as a statement that the base paper's Fig.~1 depends on simulation effects, such as queueing or mobility, that are not documented; it is not evidence that the figure is wrong. The other series belong to uncited schemes, the y-axis is ``communication overhead (\%)'' under a ``processing'' caption with an undefined baseline, and a model with enough free parameters would match any smooth curves and show nothing, so we did not fit one. To explore what such effects could do we built our own OMNeT++ model of the base paper's Table~II setting (\path{research/reference_replication/}). Its inputs include several guesses (adversary rate, server enrolment, arrival window), so we treat it as a sensitivity analysis and not as evidence about the authors' simulation or figures. As an illustration of that dependence only: over the guessed inputs we varied, the mean authentication latency of the proposed scheme at 1000 devices ranges from about 2~ms to 5.6~s, so a rising curve like the base paper's Fig.~1 is possible but its size is not determined by the paper's parameters. Consequently, \textbf{every claim below about processing cost rests on the base paper's Table~V and our measured constants, not on the base paper's Fig.~1,} and we make no claim about the correctness of the base paper's Fig.~1 or about the 21\% figure beyond its non-derivability from the tables.

\subsection{Measured primitive costs}
\label{sec:constants}
Table~\ref{tab:constants} compares the paper's constants with ours; Fig.~\ref{fig:constants} plots the ESP8266 values. On the ESP8266 at 80\,MHz a P-256 scalar multiplication takes 276\,ms (the fastest implementation available in its BearSSL core; generic code takes 624\,ms and X25519 131\,ms), sixteen times the assumed 17.1\,ms, while SHA-1 and AES-GCM are 9$\times$ and 5--12$\times$ \emph{faster} than assumed. The ratio that decides most rankings, $T_{ECM}/T_H$, is 7600 on the device against the paper's 53. Timing spread is negligible on the microcontroller (the 25th and 75th percentiles equal the median), and ratios are identical at 160\,MHz.

\textbf{Against ECC-based schemes} the proposed scheme is far cheaper than the paper states. On the ESP8266 at 80\,MHz (device plus server, AES-GCM, \texttt{aes\_big}/\texttt{aes\_ct}) it costs 2.1/4.7\,ms, whereas Sadhukhan, Shuai and Cheng--Le cost 279--553\,ms (Shuai at 553\,ms with the paper's device ECC operation; about 277\,ms without it, see Section~\ref{sec:audit}), Challa 2487\,ms and Porambage 5251\,ms, i.e.\ about 60$\times$ (Sadhukhan against \texttt{aes\_ct}) to 2500$\times$ (Porambage against \texttt{aes\_big}) more. (Challa and Wazid include the fuzzy-extraction time $T_{fe}$, which we did not measure and keep at the paper's value.)

\textbf{Against the hash-only rows} the outcome depends on whether the session KDF is counted. The base paper's Table~V has three such rows: Turkanovi\'c et al., Dhillon--Kalra, and the row labelled ``Jiang'', which appears to be the two-factor scheme of Jiang et al.~\cite{jiang2} (Section~\ref{sec:audit}). The first two are confirmed hash-and-XOR-only schemes by the independent tabulations of~\cite{changle,dhillon}; the third only by a survey table~\cite{zhong}. The proposed scheme beats a hash-only row only if $r=T_{SE/D}/T_H$ is below roughly 2.0--4.5, depending on the cost column; the paper's own constants give $r=17.5$. With the base paper's Table~V as printed (V1), the hash-only rows are cheaper in all 12 AES-GCM sensitivity cells on the ESP8266 at both clocks ($r=6.7$--31.6, i.e.\ 3.8--10.8$\times$ cheaper), in 11 of 12 on the Graviton2 core, and in 6 of 12 on the laptop. But measured HKDF-SHA256 costs $6$--$17\,T_H$ on every platform, against $5$--$7\,T_H$ for the hash-only device columns as printed ($5$--$6$ once Turkanovi\'c et al.'s swapped device and server counts are corrected), so a device that must run the session KDF already costs more than they do before any encryption. Table~\ref{tab:variants} shows that counting the KDF (V2--V4) makes all three rows cheaper than the proposed scheme in every column on every platform we measured, including OpenSSL's hardware AES and SHA (GCM 78\,ns, SHA-1 38\,ns, HKDF 232\,ns per call); on the ESP8266 the gap is 3.8--13.7$\times$ across V1--V4. The ranking flips only if $T_{SE/D}$ is read as unauthenticated AES (one block or CTR without a MAC), which drops the MAC the scheme requires. The operation-count model also under-predicts the implementation: it gives 2.4\,ms of device compute (\texttt{aes\_ct}) where we measure 5.3\,ms.

\textbf{The chaotic-map scheme of Jiang et al.~\cite{jiang}, as published, is not a hash-only scheme.} Its authentication phase alone needs $7T_H+4T_C$ ($\approx131$\,ms with its own $T_C=32.40$\,ms), and login plus authentication need $11T_H+6T_C$; on the ESP8266 the latter costs about 195\,ms against 1--35\,ms for the proposed scheme. It would be cheaper than the proposed scheme only if $T_C$ were below a crossover of 0.14--5.2\,ms (0.28--0.49\,ms on the 80\,MHz ESP8266, depending on the reading of the proposed scheme), which we did not measure. The conclusion that the proposed scheme costs more than its hash-only competitors therefore stands firmly for Turkanovi\'c et al.\ and Dhillon--Kalra. For the ``Jiang'' row it holds to the extent that the survey's counts for the two-factor scheme~\cite{jiang2}, which is a different scheme from the chaotic-map one, are right; the row is not affected by the $T_C$ discussion above.


\subsection{Protocol-level observations}
\label{sec:protocol}
\textbf{No forward secrecy.} The session key depends on $k^\star$, $N_i$, $N_j$ and $t$ (Eq.~(16)); $N_i$, $N_j$ and $t$ travel encrypted only under $k^\star$. An attacker who later obtains $k^\star$ recovered all 5 of 5 past session keys from recorded $M_1$/$M_2$ alone. Chang and Le's Table~IV~\cite{changle} lists no forward secrecy for Turkanovi\'c et al.\ either, so against that scheme the comparison is like for like; their second protocol and Jiang et al.\ claim it, so against the ECC-based schemes it is not.

\textbf{ECC is not used online, and is nominal in the base paper's Section~III model.} Eq.~(8) defines $\lambda_X$ as a hash reduced modulo $n$, not a curve operation; the public point $P_X=\lambda_X G$ is set only optionally and Eqs.~(9)--(22) never use it. (The registration in Section~II of the base paper does run an ECDH exchange with the trusted authority, Eqs.~(1)--(5); it is offline, belongs to the other description of the protocol, and we did not implement it.) Every online operation is a hash, KDF, encryption or MAC.

\textbf{Server cost grows with the devices to be resolved.} Because $\mathrm{PID}=H_2(\lambda\,\|\,t)$ is unlinkable, the server cannot index by pseudonym and must probe (devices $\times$ epoch window). On the laptop one resolution took 4.8\,ms at 1000 and 23.9\,ms at 5000 devices (a 13-epoch window), against the constant server cost of the base paper's Table~V. A precomputed table avoids the compute but needs $1000\times13\times32$\,B $\approx 416$\,KB at 1000 devices against the paper's 320-bit server storage (the base paper's Table~VI): either way the scheme pays $O(D\cdot W)$ for $D$ devices to resolve and window $W$. The paper does not say how many devices a server must resolve, so this is a scaling observation, not a magnitude.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
\textbf{What the evidence supports.} The paper's advantage over ECC-based schemes is real and larger than reported, but those schemes may provide different properties (forward secrecy, Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). Its processing advantage over hash-only schemes is not supported once the session KDF that its own equations require is counted. Its communication and storage claims cannot be checked, because the paper's tables and text disagree and the competitor rows could be traced to their sources only in part (two through secondary tables). Its simulated figures are neither confirmed nor refuted here.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} We measured one microcontroller class (32-bit); the paper's targets include 8- and 16-bit parts, and we did not measure an MCU with AES or SHA hardware or energy. The ESP8266 timings were identical to the microsecond on two boards and across repeated runs; the laptop's absolute times shifted by 1.3--2.3$\times$ in Low Power Mode, so we rely on ratios there and recorded the power state of the rerun. Primitive costs are BearSSL's and OpenSSL's; other libraries shift constants, although the KDF result depends on HKDF costing several hash operations, not on absolute speed. A hardware HKDF/HMAC engine could change it. Competitor costs use the operation counts of~\cite{base}. We checked them against Chang and Le, Dhillon and Kalra, and Jiang et al.; Turkanovi\'c et al.\ is confirmed only through the two papers that tabulate it, for the ``Jiang'' and ``Shuai'' rows we had only secondary tabulations~\cite{zhong,banerjee} (Section~\ref{sec:audit}), and for bit sizes only the Shuai and Fakroon totals, which match the byte counts of~\cite{banerjee}; we had no source for any storage entry. $T_C$ and $T_{fe}$ were not measured, and our own OMNeT++ model is a sensitivity analysis only (Section~\ref{sec:fig1}). The elliptic-curve benchmark ran on a temporary heap stack on the ESP8266 (the 4\,KB task stack is too small), with outputs verified by checksum. The t4g.nano is a shared-host cloud VM pinned to one core, so its absolute numbers are indicative only, and it has no hardware-crypto measurement. Tables were transcribed from rendered PDFs and figure values read by eye. Our implementation is one reading of a specification that leaves the epoch and replay window undefined (it is held in RAM in the Python reference; the firmware persists it to flash). We have not contacted the authors.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
Measured on real hardware, the protocol's cost model is dominated by two things the paper's constants get wrong: an ECC scalar multiplication on a small MCU is sixteen times slower than assumed, and the session-key derivation is several times more expensive than a hash. The first makes the scheme look better against ECC-based schemes than the paper claims; the second makes it look worse against hash-only schemes. The paper's stated percentages cannot be derived from its own data, and its processing figure cannot be reproduced from published information. Future work: obtain the authors' simulation scenario and rerun the base paper's Fig.~1 against it; measure an MCU with hardware AES and SHA and a Raspberry Pi~3; read the original Shuai et al.\ and two-factor Jiang et al.\ papers to confirm the secondary tabulations, and verify the remaining entries against the original papers; implement and time Turkanovi\'c et al.\ end to end on the microcontroller rather than modelling it; and complete the TA-assisted key-revocation extension we proposed earlier.
