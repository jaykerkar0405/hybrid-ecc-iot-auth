\begin{abstract}
Al-Rasheed et al.\ proposed a hybrid elliptic-curve/symmetric-key mutual-authentication protocol for IoT devices and evaluated it in OMNeT++ without releasing code. Since neither code nor simulation model is available, we ask what can be established independently. We provide an open reference implementation, a C port of its device side that runs on an ESP8266, and independent reference vectors that both implementations reproduce byte for byte while rejecting tampered and replayed messages. Checking the paper against itself, we find that its tables disagree with its text (four messages and 1024 bits against two messages and 264 bits), that its headline savings of about 17\%, 21\% and 12\% cannot be derived from its Tables~IV--VI, and that its constants and comparison rows also appear in an earlier paper. We then model the stated environment (its Table~II network and Table~III per-operation times) in OMNeT++: the model reproduces the paper's per-session cost of $2T_H+2T_{SE/D}$ per side (23.68\,ms by the paper's arithmetic, 23.98\,ms simulated) and the sizes of its Table~IV. Timing the primitives on the ESP8266 and re-running the model lowers the cost to 2.25\,ms, and the server-side pseudonym lookup, which the paper leaves open, decides scalability: flat with a hash index, growing with the number of devices under a linear scan. The paper's simulated figures cannot be checked without the authors' model, which we have requested. The implementation, model, raw hardware logs and scripts are open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Lightweight mutual authentication for resource-constrained devices is commonly evaluated by counting cryptographic operations and weighting them with per-operation times. Al-Rasheed et al.~\cite{base} propose a protocol in which devices and servers share long-term secrets from an offline phase and authenticate online with symmetric primitives only, and they evaluate it in OMNeT++ against other schemes. No implementation or simulation project accompanies the paper.

This paper contributes (i)~an open reference implementation of the protocol (Python, AES-GCM, HKDF-SHA256, SHA-256) and a portable C device core that runs on an ESP8266 and interoperates with the Python server; (ii)~independent reference vectors, computed without any of the implementation's code, that both implementations reproduce byte for byte, together with an explicit register of the decisions the paper leaves open; (iii)~a consistency check of the paper's tables against its own text and references, which does not depend on our implementation; (iv)~an OMNeT++ model of the paper's stated environment, parameterised only by its Tables~II--V, and a check that implementation and model reproduce its per-session cost and communication sizes; (v)~hardware timings of the primitives behind the paper's cost constants, and the same model re-run with them; and (vi)~a scalability study of the server-side pseudonym lookup, which the paper does not specify.

\section{Protocol and Implementation}
We implement the nonce-based exchange of Section~III of~\cite{base}. A trusted authority (TA) derives each entity's long-term secret $\lambda_X=H_1(ID_X\,\|\,s)$ locally and distributes pairwise keys $k^\star_{ij}$ in an offline phase. In an epoch $t$ a device $V_i$ derives a pseudonym $PID_{V_i,t}=H_2(\lambda_{V_i}\,\|\,t)$, samples a nonce $N_i$, and sends
\begin{gather*}
M_1=\langle PID_{V_i,t},\,C_1,\,\mu_1\rangle,\\
(C_1,\mu_1)=\mathrm{AEAD}_{k^\star_{ij}}(PID_{V_i,t}\,\|\,N_i\,\|\,t).
\end{gather*}
The server resolves the pseudonym to a known device and epoch, verifies and decrypts $M_1$, samples $N_j$, answers with $M_2$ (the same construction), and both sides derive $K_{ij,t}=\mathrm{KDF}(k^\star_{ij}\,\|\,N_i\,\|\,N_j\,\|\,t)$. Replayed messages are rejected by an epoch window per peer. We instantiate AEAD as AES-256-GCM~\cite{gcm}, KDF as HKDF-SHA256~\cite{hkdf} and $H$ as SHA-256, as suggested in the instantiation notes of~\cite{base}. The wire format is a length-prefixed JSON/base64 envelope.

The device side is also written as a portable C core on BearSSL~\cite{bearssl} with the identical wire format. It runs natively against the unmodified Python server (12 of 12 handshakes) and on a NodeMCU ESP8266 over Wi-Fi (20 of 20 handshakes). In every handshake the session-key fingerprints on the two sides were identical and a replayed $M_1$ was rejected.

\section{Conformance to the Specification}\label{sec:conf}
\subsection{Specification and assumptions}
The paper fixes the equations but not their encodings. We therefore froze one specification of Section~III of~\cite{base} (Eqs.~8--24) and classified each decision as stated by the paper, suggested by its instantiation notes, or our assumption. The assumptions cover byte encodings, HKDF parameters and domain-separation strings, the GCM IV and associated-data use, the wire format and epoch handling. The register, and a matrix from each equation to its Python and C code and to the evidence that covers it, are part of the repository. The C port implements the device half only; coverage is an abstract predicate and the optional rekeying of Eq.~24 is a stub.

\subsection{Independent reference vectors}
A generator that imports nothing from the implementation restates the specification using the Python standard library and a direct AES-256-GCM call with chosen IVs. It first checks its own primitives against RFC~5869 (cases A.1 and A.3)~\cite{hkdf}, NIST GCM test case~16~\cite{gcm} and the FIPS~180 SHA-256 vector for ``abc''~\cite{fips180}. It then fixes the identities, master secret, epochs, protocol nonces and GCM IVs for two epochs, and computes the long-term secrets, pairwise root, pseudonym, plaintexts, ciphertexts, tags, wire bytes and session key. The implementations must reproduce every value (Table~\ref{tab:vectors}).

\subsection{What this does and does not show}
The vectors show that both implementations agree with an independent restatement of the specification, so a mistake in code they share would be caught. They do not show that the specification is the authors': five questions remain that only they can answer (the associated data covered by $\mu_1$, the construction of $\sigma_2$, how epochs advance and persist, how a server resolves a pseudonym, and what enrolment distributes). Nor are they a security proof.

\section{Evaluation Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\subsection{Environment and constants}
The OMNeT++ model uses the parameters of Table~II of~\cite{base}: a $1500\times1500$\,m area, 100 to 1000 devices, servers at 5\% of the device count, a 10\,Mbps channel, 75.6\,mW transmit and receive power, a 15\,$\mu$s time quantum, and a 500\,m range. Operation times are those of its Table~III (``reference values for the processing time''): $T_H=0.32$\,ms and $T_{SE/D}=5.6$\,ms. Operation counts and message sizes come from its Tables~IV and~V, and no parameter is fitted to any result. These tables are not original measurements of the proposed protocol: the five constants of its Table~III, the nine competitor rows of its Table~IV and the finite entries of its Table~VI also appear, with the same numbers, in Tables~3, 5 and~6 of Sadhukhan et al.~\cite{sadhukhan}, which it cites as~[18]. In~\cite{sadhukhan} the storage columns are headed smart card/device and sensor node, whereas~\cite{base} labels the same numbers server and device storage, and an arithmetic slip in~\cite{sadhukhan} (32 + 320 bits stated as 356 instead of 352) is carried over. We observe the correspondence and draw no conclusion about how the values were obtained. The model is written in plain OMNeT++~\cite{omnet} 6.4.0 (no INET, no SUMO) and is our own implementation of the stated environment.

\subsection{Assumptions the paper does not fix}\label{sec:assume}
The paper leaves several inputs open, and we state ours: an adversary share of 5\% of the devices, each sending forged requests at a mean rate of 1\,Hz; an authentication window of 10\,s in which every device authenticates once at a uniform time; every server resolves every device in its range; and a first-come-first-served server CPU. Devices with no server in range cannot authenticate and are counted separately. Each result is the mean of five seeds. The assumption most relevant to the protocol is how a server resolves a pseudonym (Section~\ref{sec:lookup}).

\subsection{Hardware testbed}
Primitive timings were taken on a NodeMCU ESP8266 (Tensilica L106, stock 80\,MHz and 160\,MHz, about 80\,KB RAM, no cryptographic hardware, Wi-Fi off) with BearSSL, timed by the cycle counter; each figure is the median of 100 runs (24 for elliptic-curve operations). Every benchmark output (hash, AES block, CTR, GCM ciphertext and tag, curve points) was checksummed, and all 35 digests are identical on both clocks and on a laptop running the same code. The whole benchmark was repeated on a second ESP8266 board: the 35 medians agree with the first board and with the earlier run to the microsecond.

\subsection{Simulation with measured constants}\label{sec:measuredsim}
Re-running the model with the ESP8266 constants (Table~\ref{tab:session}, right) puts the per-session cost of the proposed scheme at 2.25\,ms with Table~V's counts and 3.51\,ms with the session KDF, against 23.98\,ms and 35.18\,ms with Table~III. With the hash index the latency is flat at the cost-model value. \subsection{Reproducibility}
The model's 3,385-run main sweep was run on two toolchains (OMNeT++ 6.0.3 on Linux and 6.4.0 on macOS arm64); all rows agree to a relative difference of $5\times10^{-14}$. The 495 hash-index and uncontended runs were added on macOS only. The hardware benchmark is archived with raw serial logs for both boards and both clocks, and the C device core interoperates with the Python server on the same wire format. Scripts, configuration generators, result tables and the figure script are open, as is the reference-vector generator; everything is in the project repository (\url{https://github.com/jaykerkar0405/hybrid-ecc-iot-auth}).

\section{Limitations}
The model is ours, not the authors'; the assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:assume} are not specified by the paper and results at saturation depend on them. The results of Sections~\ref{sec:persession}, \ref{sec:measuredsim} and~\ref{sec:lookup} are model output, not measurements, and those under Table~III constants use the paper's assumed values. Conformance (Section~\ref{sec:conf}) is to our reading of the specification, which only the authors can confirm. Hardware timings come from one microcontroller family with software cryptography and one library; other devices, libraries or hardware AES will differ, and 8- and 16-bit parts were not measured. The end-to-end firmware figures are means over 20 handshakes recorded in our project repository; the per-primitive benchmarks, unlike them, are archived with raw logs. Constants without a measured counterpart ($T_C$, $T_{fe}$) are taken from~\cite{base}, which reuses those of~\cite{sadhukhan}. The other schemes of its tables are not re-implemented, and their operation counts are taken from the paper, checked against the original papers where we could obtain them. We did not evaluate the figures of~\cite{base}, whose underlying data is not published. We emailed its corresponding author on 27~October 2026 to request the simulation model and the source of the Table~III constants; no reply had arrived by 30~October 2026, and anything that depends on their simulation remains unverified.

\section{Conclusion}
We provide an open implementation, an ESP8266 port and an OMNeT++ model of the protocol of~\cite{base}, and independent reference vectors that both implementations reproduce. Checking the paper against itself shows that its tables contradict its text and that its headline savings cannot be derived from them. Under the paper's stated environment and constants, our model reproduces its per-session processing cost and communication sizes. Timing the primitives on a constrained device and re-running the model with the measured constants lowers the per-session cost by an order of magnitude, and the server-side pseudonym resolution, which the paper leaves open, determines how the protocol scales with the number of devices. Confirming the remaining points requires the authors' simulation model and their answers to the open specification questions.
